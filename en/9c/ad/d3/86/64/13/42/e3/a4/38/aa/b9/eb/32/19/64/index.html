What Is a Landing Page?

By @danny · Tutorial

A landing page is a web page built with a single objective in mind: that the visitor does one thing. It might be buying, subscribing to an email list, downloading something, requesting a quote or signing up. Everything on that page pushes toward that one action. Nothing else.

The idea in an example

Imagine you're launching an online course. You can have a full site with a blog, a separate pricing section, an "about me" page and the whole informational website. But to sell the course you're going to send people to a page that only says: what the course offers, why it's good for you, how much it costs, and a giant "Buy" button. That's a landing page.

The name comes from there: the visitor "lands" on that page from an ad, social media, a link or a search, and is expected to perform the conversion it was designed for.

When is a landing page a good idea?

They're used all the time in marketing campaigns. A few typical cases:

  • Subscription: collecting emails by promising something free (a PDF, a discount, access to a webinar).
  • Direct sale: a product or course with its payment button.
  • Event registration: getting people to sign up for a workshop or a talk.
  • Product apps: when you launch an app and ask people to register or download it.
  • Testing hypotheses: you launch a quick page to see if people are interested in the idea before spending time developing it.

The simple rule is: if you want the visitor to take one single action, point everything at a landing page. If you want them to explore and browse, that's not a landing page, that's a normal site.

What parts a landing page has

Although each one is different, almost all are put together with the same elements, one after another:

  1. Headline: the promise in one short sentence. It grabs attention in the first few seconds.
  2. Subtitle and offer: a little more detail about what you get.
  3. CTA (call to action): the button people should press. "Buy now", "Download", "Sign up".
  4. Social proof: testimonials, reviews, customer numbers. They build trust.
  5. Form or payment option: the final step to complete the action.
  6. Trust elements: refund guarantee, contact details, policies.

The length depends on the product. For something cheap and simple, a short landing page. For an expensive purchase, more text, more proof and more explanation are justified.

Landing page or normal web page?

The difference is one of focus. A website has several goals: informing, ranking in search engines, showing the company, getting people to browse. A landing page has only one and is optimized to achieve it, removing distractions.

That's why on a landing page you avoid putting the full menu, links that take the visitor away, or long texts that don't contribute to conversion. The whole design guides the eye toward the button.

What it's built with

Technically it's not a special "type" of software: it's HTML, CSS and JavaScript like any other page. What changes is the strategy. You can make it by hand, with a framework like React, or with visual block-based tools (if you don't know how to program). What matters isn't the tool, but that it's fast, looks good on mobile and has an extremely clear CTA.

And one key thing: a good landing page is measured. If you run ad campaigns, you can test two versions (change the title, the button, the color) and keep the one that converts best. That's called A/B testing and it's part of the day-to-day for anyone doing digital marketing.

Before creating a landing page, ask yourself: what exactly do I want the person to do when they get here? If the answer is clear, the rest is arranging the page around that action. One single idea, one single button, zero distractions. That, is the art of the landing page.
